## 6. Autocorrelation of the target (stormflow_mgd)

The autocorrelation function (ACF) measures how similar the series is to itself shifted in time. It is relevant for this project because:

- If ACF(lag=1) is very close to 1, predicting the next step is almost trivial by copying the current value. This explains the high NSE at H=1.

- The speed at which ACF decays toward zero indicates how much useful prediction horizon the series has. If ACF drops quickly, predicting at H=6 is intrinsically difficult regardless of the model.

### ACF at lags of interest (train-only)

| Lag (steps) | Lag (minutes) | ACF |
|-------------|---------------|----:|
| 1 | 5 | 0.9087 |
| 3 | 15 | 0.7157 |
| 6 | 30 | 0.5559 |
| 12 | 60 | 0.4207 |
| 24 | 120 | 0.2751 |
| 36 | 180 | 0.2261 |
| 72 | 360 | 0.1404 |
| 144 | 720 | 0.0937 |
| 288 | 1440 | 0.0431 |

> **Reading:** ACF at 5 minutes = 0.909, ACF at 30 minutes = 0.556. As a theoretical approximation, the NSE of an AR(1) persistence predictor would be lower-bounded by 2*rho - 1 = 0.817 at H=1 and 0.112 at H=6. The exact empirical value on test, computed in section 8 of this document, is **NSE naive = 0.811 at H=1**. The empirical figure in section 8 is the authoritative one; the approximations here only provide context for how the signal decays in the series.

## 8. Comparison with naive predictor (persistence)

The current model is compared with a trivial predictor that copies the current value as the prediction for the next step. Formally: $\hat{y}(t+h) = y(t)$. This is the absolute baseline: any useful model must clearly outperform it.

The comparison is especially relevant because section 6 shows that the target ACF is high at short horizons (0.91 at 5 min), which implies that persistence already has non-trivial performance.

### Model NSE vs naive NSE (test split)

| Horizon | Min | NSE naive | NSE model SIN SF | Gain SIN SF | NSE model CON SF | Gain CON SF |
|-----------|-----|----------:|------------------:|----------------:|------------------:|----------------:|
| H=1 | 5m | 0.811 | 0.861 | +0.050 | 0.854 | +0.043 |
| H=3 | 15m | 0.409 | 0.471 | +0.062 | 0.488 | +0.079 |
| H=6 | 30m | 0.081 | -1.212 | -1.293 | 0.255 | +0.174 |

### Reading

- **H=1 (5 min):** the SIN SF model outperforms the naive baseline by +0.050 NSE. Moderate improvement.
- **H=3 (15 min):** the SIN SF model outperforms the naive baseline by +0.062 NSE.
- **H=6 (30 min):** the SIN SF model is CATASTROPHICALLY worse than the naive baseline (1.29 NSE difference).

> **Operational and academic implication:** reporting NSE=0.861 at H=1 without comparing it against the naive baseline gives a misleading impression of the value added by the model. A robust TFM defense must include this comparison and justify why a model with ~104K parameters improves (or not) over a one-line predictor.

## 9. Synthesis of findings for the external review

This section summarizes the most relevant findings from the document, intended to serve as a starting point for the review with Opus 4.7. It introduces no new analysis: it makes explicit what sections 1-8 already show.

### Findings ordered by severity

**1. [HIGH] The model barely outperforms the naive predictor at H=1 and is WORSE at H=3 and H=6.**

Gain of the SIN SF model over persistence: H=1 +0.050, H=3 +0.062, H=6 -1.293.
A model with ~104K parameters must justify why it improves (or not) over a one-line predictor.
Question for the reviewer: is NSE=0.861 at H=1 really a good result considering that naive gives 0.811?

**2. [HIGH] Asymmetry across splits in extreme event coverage.**

Maximum value by split: train=199.4 MGD, **val=225.3 MGD** (absolute maximum of the dataset), test=135.2 MGD.
Extremes by split: train=677, val=97, test=59.
Consequence: the model is evaluated on test under a less extreme regime than training, while val includes the absolute peak. Early stopping on val may be favoring underestimation.

**3. [MEDIUM] Insufficient sample size for the Extreme bucket in test.**

Only 59 extreme samples in test. Metrics on this bucket (NSE=-0.99, bias=-12.7 MGD) have high variance. A single outlier event can shift them substantially.
Question for the reviewer: does it make sense to report metrics on this bucket with this sample, or is it better to use bootstrap or k-fold to estimate confidence intervals?

**4. [MEDIUM] Massive redundancy among features.**

36 pairs of features with |Pearson| >= 0.7. The most extreme: rain_sum_10m vs rain_max_10m (0.985), rain_sum_10m vs rain_sum_15m (0.970), api_dynamic correlates with 9 different features.
Of the 22 declared features, the effective number of independent dimensions is much smaller (possibly ~8-10).
Question for the reviewer: is it worth reducing features before continuing to iterate on architecture/loss?

**5. [MEDIUM] The target ACF imposes a theoretical limit at H>1.**

ACF(5 min) = 0.909, ACF(30 min) = 0.556. The ACF decays quickly after the first 30 minutes.
This indicates that, without incorporating external rainfall prediction (option c in STATE.md), extending the useful horizon beyond ~15 min may be physically limited.

### Suggested priorities (to validate with the reviewer)

1. **Report the naive baseline in all future metrics.** No-regrets, 5 lines of code.
2. **Review the split.** Evaluate alternative splits (full years, TimeSeriesSplit, blocked CV by events).
3. **Simplify features.** Reduce from 22 to ~10 by removing redundancy, with permutation importance on the simplified model.
4. **Confidence intervals.** Bootstrap on test to quantify metric uncertainty in the Extreme bucket.
5. **Realistic horizon.** Accept that H>1 with the current features has a physical limit, or integrate the MSD rainfall model (option c).
